## 3. Verdict

**Already done (do not claim as a contribution):**
- **H1 as a standalone claim.** Bowen et al. already showed name- and location-proxy disparities in LLM mortgage underwriting across vendors and generations, and Social Group Bias in AI Finance and several name audits support it. The plan's fairness-instruction mitigation is also done (Bowen; Tamkin). In this paper H1 is only a gating step that produces flipped pairs.
- **Measuring disclosure conditional on a flip** (Chen et al.; Turpin et al.).
- **"CoT hides demographic bias in a high-stakes decision"** in general. Karvonen & Marks already report 0% disclosure in hiring. H2 in lending is a domain transfer.

**Incremental (publishable only with strong execution):**
- **H2 in lending**, with outputs that matter legally (adverse-action letters, Reg B reason codes) rather than CoT. The adverse-action framing is a real FAccT and ICAIF angle, but on its own it reads as "Karvonen & Marks for mortgages".
- **H3 pretext test.** Fixing the cited reason and checking whether the decision changes is a lending version of Matton et al.'s causal concept faithfulness. It becomes more valuable if it is tied explicitly to the Reg B "factors actually considered" standard.

**Plausibly novel (lead with this):**
- **H4 together with Setups 2 and 3**: an **executed** deterministic scoring tool, with (a) the rate at which the LLM overrides the tool, broken down by proxy, and (b) **proxy-conditioned drift in explanations when the decision is fixed** (same tool decision and same tool reason codes, different name or ZIP, giving different letter content, tone, reasons or remediation advice). No retrieved work tests this. AgentFairBench explicitly defers executed tools and rationales to v2, and Geng et al. defer decoy attributions. This is the paper's best claim to novelty and also its most deployment-relevant one, since banks will use LLMs as narrators of scorecards.

**Biggest empirical risk.** Recent evidence points to small or null H1 effects in current aligned models: Tripathy & Buckmann find parity, Vohra & Ravikiran find no surviving contrasts, AgentFairBench finds mostly nulls, and Haim et al. find that numeric anchors cancel bias. With about 150 profiles and one run per variant, flipped pairs may be too few to estimate H2 or H3 disclosure rates. The plan's pilot go/no-go point is right, but it needs a pre-committed fallback (see 4c).

## 4. Suggestions to sharpen novelty and design

### 4a. Reframe the headline
1. Lead with **"Fixed decision, biased narrative: auditing LLM-written adverse-action reasons around a deterministic scorecard"** (Setup 3 and H4), with Setup 2 as the main override result. Setup 1, H1 and H2 then become motivation and replication of Bowen and Karvonen & Marks.
2. Turn H4 into **measurable sub-hypotheses**: (H4a) override rate differs by proxy; (H4b) given the same tool decision and reason codes, the letter's cited reasons differ from the tool's codes more often for one group (a reason-fidelity gap); (H4c) tone and remediation differ (e.g., "consider a co-signer" or "improve financial habits" vs. neutral wording). H4b is a direct test of the CFPB Circular 2023-03 "closest identifiable factor" violation. Score it against the tool's own top-k reason codes, as Geng et al. do with SHAP.
3. Present H3 as a **causal concept faithfulness** test (cite Matton et al.) and borrow their counterfactual-intervention framing so reviewers don't see it as ad hoc.

### 4b. Confounds and validity threats in the plan
4. **Names signal more than race.** Audit names also signal class, education, age, familiarity and nativity. AgentFairBench's own probe confirms this, and it found Hispanic names read as foreign-born 70–100% of the time. Fixes: use names from Crabtree et al. 2023 with published perception scores (as Bowen does); match on perceived income and education; use several names per group (≥10) and include name as a random effect; run a **within-race placebo pair** (two White names, two Black names) to estimate how often names alone cause flips; run a perception probe. Report results as "name-bundle effects", not "race effects".
5. **ZIP code may stand for real credit risk, and models may be right to use it.** Even with tract median income matched, tracts differ in house-price volatility, foreclosure history, flood/insurance risk, LTV appraisal risk and lender density. A ZIP-based denial may be legitimate collateral or geographic risk rather than a pretext, and under fair-lending law geography is a redlining issue, not a clean protected attribute. Fixes: match tracts on income, **HPI trend, owner-occupancy rate, median property value and the HMDA denial rate for White applicants**; tell the model that the property appraisal and collateral are identical; add a **within-race ZIP placebo** (two majority-White tracts with similar incomes); report the ZIP and name arms separately and do not pool them into one "bias" estimate.
6. **What HMDA actually contains.** Public LAR data has DTI only in **bands** (e.g., "<20%", "20%-<30%", "30%-<36%", then single values 36–49, then "50%-60%", ">60%"), CLTV, loan amount rounded to the 10k midpoint, income in $000s, property value, tract, loan type and purpose, action taken, and up to 4 **denial reasons**. It does **not** contain credit score (only the score *model* type), employment, savings or names, and several fields are exempt for small filers. Implications: (i) most of the decision-relevant signal, especially credit score, is synthetic, so "built from HMDA" adds less realism than it suggests. Say this plainly and check the synthetic score against HMDA denial-reason frequencies. (ii) Because DTI is reported as single values in 36–49, the plan's "fill exact DTI within band" step hardly applies in that range. (iii) Use HMDA's **denial-reason field** as an external benchmark for the reason distribution the LLM cites. That is a cheap, novel validation.
7. **Borderline-only sampling.** Keeping only DTI 40–49 gets the most flips but limits external validity and invites "you chose the cases most likely to flip". Add a small stratum of clear approvals and clear denials as negative controls, and report results by stratum.
8. **Sampling noise vs. proxy flips.** "One run per variant, reruns only for flipped pairs" biases flip estimates, since reruns are triggered by the outcome. Run **k≥3 replicates for every variant** in the pilot to estimate a test-retest flip floor, include a **control-vs-control pair** (same neutral name, run twice), and define a flip as a proxy effect beyond that floor (cf. the normalization in Chen et al.; AgentFairBench's arity correction if you compare more than 2 groups). Use McNemar or conditional logit at the pair level.
9. **Control condition.** "Neutral name, no ZIP" differs from the treatment arms in **two** ways (name present/absent and ZIP present/absent). Compare White-name vs. Black-name and tract A vs. tract B **directly**, keeping the same fields in both, and treat the "no-proxy" arm as a separate anchor.
10. **Audit awareness.** Vohra & Ravikiran report that models recognize transparent audits. Avoid side-by-side pairs, randomize field order, put the name in a realistic application header, and vary irrelevant fields (a hobby or job title) as a placebo factor.
11. **Disclosure judge validity.** In letters to applicants, models will almost never write "because of your name", partly because it is illegal and partly because of style. So "non-disclosure" may reflect the output format rather than hidden reasoning. Measure disclosure on the **reasoning trace or summary** (thinking-on models), and **separately** on the letter. Also add an "ask afterwards" probe ("Did the applicant's name or neighborhood affect your decision?"), following Turpin and Chen. Validate the judge against Gowtham's labels with κ and report it. Note that OpenAI only exposes reasoning **summaries**, so H2 is not comparable across vendors. State that limitation.
12. **H3 operationalization.** "Fix the cited reason and see if the decision changes" needs a clear counterfactual. For example, if the cited reason is "DTI too high", lower the DTI by a pre-set amount in **both** members of the pair and check whether the gap remains. Pre-register the size of the fix. Also count "inconsistent reasons": the two members of a pair have identical values, yet the denied one is told that value is disqualifying. This deterministic check needs no judge.
13. **Setup 2 design.** Specify what the tool returns (score only, score plus decision, or score plus reason codes). The amount of override and leakage depends heavily on this. Vary it as a factor if budget allows (it is cheap, since it changes only the prompt). Log whether the tool was actually called. Not calling it is itself a proxy-conditioned behavior worth reporting.
14. **Statistics and power.** With n≈150 and flip rates possibly around 2–5%, you expect only about 3–8 flipped pairs per proxy and model, which is too few for disclosure rates with useful CIs. Pre-register a minimum flipped-pair count, and pool across models for H2 with model as a random effect. Report effect sizes such as Bowen's "credit-score-point equivalent".

### 4c. Fallbacks if H1 is weak (likely, given P2/O3/O4)
15. **Induce bias in a realistic way** and study the explanations afterwards. Use realistic bank context (as Karvonen & Marks do), or neutral-sounding "portfolio-loss anchoring" or "socio-geographic coherence" system prompts like those found by Tripathy & Buckmann's attacker. This turns H2 and H3 into "when bias is present, do letters reveal it?", which is still novel in lending and does not need natural flips.
16. Setup 3 does not need H1 at all, because explanation drift with a fixed decision is measurable even when decisions never flip. Make this the guaranteed core result.
